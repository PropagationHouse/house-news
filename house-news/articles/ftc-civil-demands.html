The Fifty-Page Question

The Federal Trade Commission sent civil investigative demands to OpenAI and Anthropic, each more than fifty pages long, seeking records of safety incidents, safeguards and marketing claims. The subpoenas of the hearing room have been replaced by the paperwork of the file.

By Propagation House·Friday, October 16, 2026·6 min read

Fifty pages each, and only the beginning of the questions. Photograph by house.

On October 14 and 15, the Federal Trade Commission issued civil investigative demands — the agency’s version of a subpoena — to OpenAI and Anthropic, according to reports from Axios and Reuters. Each demand runs to more than fifty pages. Together they ask for records of security and safety incidents, the safeguards built around the products, the companies’ privacy policies and the marketing claims made about what the systems will and will not do. The agency also plans to compel senior executives at both companies to testify.

A civil investigative demand is a document with a deadline attached. It does not accuse anyone of anything — the FTC has been explicit that issuing one establishes no violation of federal law and guarantees no enforcement action to follow. What it does is move a conversation from the press release into the record: what did you know about these incidents, when did you know it, what did you tell users, and what did you tell the market.

What the Agency Is Asking

The demands seek material on incidents in which the companies’ systems acted unpredictably or reached into external systems without authorization — reported cases of agents touching websites and government services. They seek the internal account of how those episodes were handled, what safeguards existed beforehand and what changed afterward, and how the products were described to the public in the meantime. The FTC also expects to send demands to third-party organizations, with the AI evaluation group METR named among the candidates for testimony.

A subpoena in the hearing room is a question asked in public. A fifty-page demand is a question asked on the record, with a filing cabinet attached.

The distinction matters for how the next year is likely to go. Hearings produce sound bites and letters produce replies. A civil investigative demand produces a production schedule, and production schedules produce documents — internal documents, dated and contextualized, drawn from the same systems whose external claims are under examination. The gap between what a company says about its safeguards and what its incident reports show is where consumer-protection cases live.

The Same Week the States Moved

The FTC’s demands arrived within a day of eight state attorneys general filing separate lawsuits against OpenAI over ChatGPT’s effects on minors, and days after the European Commission opened its first formal enforcement file under the AI Act. The three actions are legally distinct and procedurally independent. Taken together they mark something the industry has not had to answer to before: multiple jurisdictions asking the same questions in the same week, each with its own power to compel.

That coordination is not a conspiracy; it is a calendar. Regulators read the same incident reports, the same whistleblower accounts and the same testing disclosures that the companies publish voluntarily. When the voluntary disclosures are extensive enough, they become a menu. Every entry on it is a question someone with subpoena power can now ask in writing.

What Comes Next

The immediate next step is testimony: senior executives explaining their own products under the same standard that governs advertising claims. Executives have faced questions before, but usually as witnesses to an industry, not as subjects of a file with fifty pages of specific incidents already assembled. After testimony comes the production, and after production comes a decision — close the file or escalate it. Neither outcome will be quick, and neither will be quiet.

For OpenAI the demands extend a perimeter that already includes wrongful-death actions, copyright litigation and eight state consumer-protection suits. For Anthropic they arrive in the quarter it is preparing to tell investors about publicly. Both companies have argued publicly that regulation should be evidence-based rather than precautionary. The FTC’s response, in effect, is that evidence is exactly what it is collecting.
