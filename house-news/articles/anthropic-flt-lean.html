The Proof Compiles

Claude rebuilt the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in the Lean proof assistant — 13 million lines, roughly 29,500 verified lemmas, eleven days, six billion tokens — completing the first machine-checked verification of the 1995 result, and beating the community formalization project that had scheduled the work for 2029.

By Propagation House·Sunday, September 6, 2026·8 min read

The chalk was never checked this thoroughly. Photograph by house.

Fermat’s Last Theorem has been true since 1995, when Andrew Wiles closed a 358-year gap with a proof the world’s number theorists needed months to verify. As of Thursday it is true in a stricter sense: Anthropic announced that Claude has produced a complete, machine-checked formalization of the theorem in the Lean proof assistant — every inference compiled, nothing taken on faith. Thirteen million lines of Lean. Around 29,500 intermediate lemmas. Eleven days of largely autonomous work, at a cost of roughly six billion output tokens — between $60,000 and $300,000 at list prices, depending on whose invoice you read.

The route matters as much as the summit. Claude did not find a new proof; it rebuilt Wiles’s — the modular route through elliptic curves and Galois representations — in a formal language that permits no hand-waving. Every theorem a human mathematician had left informal, the model had to construct and verify before it could proceed. It is the first complete computer-checked verification of FLT, and it was completed before Kevin Buzzard’s own community formalization project, which had targeted 2029.

The machine did not out-mathematician Wiles. It out-patience everyone.

What a Proof Assistant Cannot Check

Buzzard’s reaction — the Imperial College mathematician behind Lean’s mathlib and the Xena project — was characteristically dry: “Anthropic has beaten me to it.” The community had assumed FLT formalization was a decade-scale grind requiring a thousand coordinated contributions. Instead, the bottleneck that took humans years — assembling the formal scaffolding beneath the summit — was absorbed by a model working in eleven days, failing, retrying, and re-deriving its way up the tower.

The caveats write themselves, and the announcement carries them honestly: this is verification, not discovery. No new mathematics came out of it; the creative act remains Wiles’s. But the economics are the story. A proof that consumed centuries of accumulated human craft is now reproducible as a bill — a five-figure render on someone’s API ledger. The demonstrable skill is not solving the unsolved; it is industrializing the already-solved. Every formal verification project in existence — cryptography, kernels, the Flyspeck catalogue — just repriced. The mathematicians who spent careers on formalization infrastructure are not obsolete this morning. They are, for the first time, outnumbered.

  • The model rebuilt Wiles’ classical route (elliptic curves, Galois representations) rather than finding a new proof — verification, not discovery.
