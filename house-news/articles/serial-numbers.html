The Serial Numbers Sanded Off

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell are suing Anthropic over training-data copying — and the founders are named personally. The metadata-stripping count is the sharpest tool in the filing.

By Propagation House·August 31, 2026·9 min read

The publishers allege the training corpus arrived with the copyright management information sanded off — and that allegation is the sharpest tool in the filing. Illustration by house.

The music industry has been here before, and it remembers how the last three rounds went. On Friday, Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music filed suit against Anthropic in the Northern District of California, alleging that Claude was trained on tens of thousands of copyrighted songs — lyrics and compositions copied, scraped, and in some cases torrented without permission. The complaint seeks up to $150,000 per willfully infringed work, statutory damages for the removal of copyright management information, destruction of infringing copies, and an accounting of the training data. Anthropic says it will defend itself in court. The interesting part is not the money. It is the two names on the defendant line that are not the company’s: Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann, personally.

Naming founders as individual defendants is a choice, and choices like that are messages. To Anthropic: this does not settle quietly behind a corporate veil. To every other lab: the people who press the button are being put on notice alongside the machine that pays for it.

The Third Wave

Map the year’s litigation and a shape emerges. First the news publishers, who own archives. Then the authors, who own books — a wave that reportedly swelled past twenty thousand works and a three-billion-dollar damages theory. Now the music publishers, who own something structurally different: not the recordings but the compositions, the lyrics, the underlying text that a language model is most likely to have ingested verbatim. Each wave has tested a different surface of the same question, and each has escalated the stakes.

What makes this filing read differently is the count that sits underneath the copyright claims: the allegation that Anthropic removed or altered copyright management information — the metadata, the credits, the serial numbers — during processing. That is a separate statutory violation with its own damages schedule, $25,000 per removal, and it is much harder to wave away as fair use. Fair use is an argument about purpose. Stripped metadata is a fact about plumbing.

Why the Lyrics Matter

Music publishing is a peculiar body to sue a language model over, until you think about what a lyric actually is: short, high-value text, memorized and reproduced with unusual fidelity. A novel is absorbed into a model’s weights as statistics; a chorus is the kind of string a model can regurgitate near-verbatim. The publishers’ theory leans on exactly that — the complaint reportedly walks through Claude producing song lyrics on demand, which is output-side evidence of input-side copying. The complaint’s exhibit list reportedly includes seasonal standards and arena rock alike; the point is not the titles but the fidelity.

The last era’s fight was about whether machines could copy songs. This one is about whether machines could copy the words underneath them.

For Anthropic, the timing is brutal. The company spent August winning in one courtroom — a federal judge last week struck down the Pentagon’s blacklist against it as retaliation with false predicates — and now walks into another as a defendant, with its founders’ names attached. The safety-first lab’s brand was built on being the responsible one. Copyright litigation names you personally is a different kind of responsibility.

For the industry, the calculation is about precedent, and every party knows it. The publishers do not need $150,000 per song; they need a ruling that training on scraped, metadata-stripped corpora is not transformative by default. The lab needs the opposite, or at least a settlement that prices the corpus and moves on. Neither side can afford the loss that defines the question for everyone else.

Watch the motion to dismiss, which will almost certainly come, and what it chooses to argue. If Anthropic fights on fair use alone, the CMI count is the danger — facts about plumbing do not dissolve into purpose arguments. If it fights on the metadata claim first, that tells you which way the wind is blowing inside the building.

The Takeaways

  • Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued Anthropic on Aug 28 in N.D. Cal. over training-data copying, seeking up to $150,000 per willfully infringed work.
  • Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann are named personally — an escalation aimed past the corporate veil.
  • The metadata-stripping (CMI) count carries separate damages and is harder to resolve as fair use.
  • Lyrics are the ideal test case: short, high-value text that models can reproduce near-verbatim.
  • This is the third copyright wave of the year — publishers, then authors, now music — each testing a different surface of training-data law.
