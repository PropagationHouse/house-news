The Province Files First

British Columbia sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in a California federal court over the Tumbler Ridge school shooting, arguing the province would not have been harmed if the company had told police what a user was saying to its model. A government is now the plaintiff.

Propagation House·Tuesday, September 22, 2026·4 min read

The claim is not that the model caused the shooting. It is that the company knew and said nothing. Photograph by house.

British Columbia filed suit against OpenAI and chief executive Sam Altman in U.S. federal court in Northern California on Monday, alleging that the company failed to warn law enforcement about violent threats a user made on ChatGPT before the Tumbler Ridge school shooting. Attorney General Niki Sharma announced the action, which seeks damages for the province's recovery costs and injunctive relief that would change how OpenAI handles violent conversations.

The province's theory is narrow and specific. It does not argue that the model caused the shooting. It argues that OpenAI had visibility into the user's activity, did not pass it to police, and that the failure to do so is the harm the province is now paying for. The distinction matters: causation is contested territory in AI litigation, but a duty to warn is a familiar legal instrument, and the province is asking a court to fit a model's output into it.

Victims and survivors had already sued OpenAI earlier this year on negligence and product-liability theories. Those cases are brought by private parties with private injuries. This one is brought by a government that spent money responding to the event - which converts the question from whether a company owed something to individuals into whether it owed something to the public.

The plaintiff changes the stakes

A province suing a model developer is a different kind of pressure than a family suing one. Private plaintiffs can be settled, dismissed, or worn down by process. A government plaintiff carries the state's own evidentiary access, a public budget for litigation, and an institutional interest in establishing the rule for everyone else in its jurisdiction.

It also arrives while the federal posture is moving in the opposite direction. Congress has drafted agent liability bills that lean on voluntary standards and contractor requirements rather than direct duties. OpenAI has publicly supported parts of that framework. A province going to federal court with a duty-to-warn claim inserts a stricter test than the legislation currently contemplates, and does it in a forum OpenAI cannot simply decline to appear in.

What the injunction would do

The damages claim is a recovery of costs, but the injunctive relief is the part that would change behavior. The province is asking the court to order OpenAI to alter how it handles violent conversations - which would mean either a new escalation path, a new notification practice, or a new record-keeping standard that makes the absence of a notification visible in advance.

None of that is exotic. It is the kind of arrangement financial institutions and platforms have lived under for years: report the threat, document the decision not to, and accept that the record will be reviewed later. The novel part is applying it to a conversational model, where the same system that produces the threat signal is also the product being sold.

OpenAI has not yet filed a response. The case now sits in a court that is already hearing frontier-lab antitrust claims and copyright claims from regional newspapers, which means the Northern District of California is quietly accumulating the industry's defining litigation in one building.

The Forum Question

Filing in Northern California rather than in Canada is a choice with consequences. A Canadian province suing in a U.S. district court submits to a judge who is already presiding over the industry's other defining cases - the frontier-lab antitrust complaint, the regional newspapers' copyright claims, the survivor suits. Whether that concentrates expertise or concentrates exposure depends on the rulings, but it does mean the same bench will decide the duty-to-warn question and the collusion question in the same building, in the same year. OpenAI has not yet answered the complaint, and the response will have to address both the harm the province claims and the existence of a duty to warn at all - which is the part of the theory that, if accepted, reaches every model developer and not just this one.
