The Store Is the Screen Now

Inside the immersive retail flagship that replaced every surface with a display — and what happens when commerce becomes a spatial interface you walk through instead of scroll.

By The Studio·July 24, 2026·12 min read

An immersive retail environment rendered in real-time across every architectural surface. Photo illustration by SIGNAL.

The first thing you notice is that nothing in the store is finished. The walls breathe. The floor shifts under your feet like sand finding its level. A mannequin turns its head to watch you pass — not a trick of the light, but a rendered figure tracking your position through the room. This is not a screen in a store. The store is the screen.

When Propagation House's spatial computing lab opened its retail flagship in March, the press called it a "mixed-reality shopping experience." That phrase is wrong in every word. There is nothing mixed about it. The entire 4,000-square-foot space — walls, ceiling, floor, shelving, product displays — is a continuous display surface. There are no products on shelves. There are no shelves.

Field Note The flagship runs on a custom render pipeline at 120fps across 8 projectors and 14 LED panels, all edge-blended into a single continuous surface.

What there is, instead, is a room that responds to you. Walk toward the back wall and the product catalog blooms outward from the point you're approaching — not a menu, not a grid, but a spatial unfolding. Items you've previously purchased or browsed online appear closer. Unknown items recede into periphery. The store is reading you, and it is rewriting itself in real time.

The Death of the Shelf

For a century, retail design has been a discipline of constraints: how many SKUs fit on a linear foot of shelving, how high can a endcap reach, what sightlines draw the eye toward impulse purchases. Every one of those constraints dissolves when the shelf is a rendered surface.

The shelf was never really the point. It was a compromise — a way to make physical inventory navigable to a human body moving through three dimensions. The shelf says: here is where this thing lives, come to it. The spatial store says: the thing lives wherever you are looking. The entire logic of wayfinding inverts. You do not go to the product. The product comes to the place you already are.

We didn't remove the shelves. We removed the assumption that a product needs a physical location to exist in a store. — Studio Lead, Spatial Computing Lab

The implications are strange. Inventory becomes a rendering problem, not a logistics one. A store can stock a million products in 4,000 square feet because none of them occupy space until a customer summons them. The flagship's backend connects directly to Propagation House's warehouse — when you reach for something, it ships. The store is a showroom for everything the company has ever made and will ever make.

What the customer actually does

The interaction model is the part nobody predicted. Early prototypes assumed customers would want to browse — scroll, filter, search. Instead, they wander.

The most common behavior, tracked across the first 90 days, is what the team calls "drift": customers walk slowly through the space, letting the environment reorganize around them. They are not looking for anything. They are letting the room look for them.

Dwell time averages 23 minutes. A conventional flagship of this size pulls 7–8. People stay because the space keeps changing, and the change is keyed to them.

This is the part that matters: people are not shopping. They are being in a room with commerce. The transaction happens, but it's almost incidental to the experience of the space. The studio's retail analysts are still arguing about whether that's a feature or a bug.

Interlude

The room does not display products. It displays intent — yours, reflected back at you in real time, rendered across every surface you can see.

The Interface Is the Architecture

Here's where it gets interesting, and where the spatial computing lab diverges from the VR/AR industry's prevailing wisdom. Every major headset manufacturer — Apple, Meta, Google — has spent the last decade trying to put a screen on your face. Propagation House's argument is that this is the wrong layer.

The headset approach puts the interface between you and the world. The room approach puts the interface as the world. You don't look through a device to see augmented reality. You walk into a space that already is one.

There is nothing on your face. There is nothing in your hand. The interface is the walls. The interface is the floor. The interface is the ceiling turning its head to watch you pass.

Contrast Apple Vision Pro: 23M pixels at 1.4kg on your face. Propagation House flagship: 8K continuous surface at 0g on your face. The math favors the room.

Key Takeaways

1
The flagship replaces all physical merchandising with continuous display surfaces — walls, floor, and ceiling included.
2
Inventory is a rendering problem, not a logistics one. The store can "stock" unlimited SKUs.
3
Customer behavior is "drift" — experiential wandering, not targeted browsing. Dwell time is 3× conventional retail.
4
The spatial approach avoids the headset problem entirely: no device, no weight, no social isolation.

What It Costs

The build was not cheap. The render pipeline, projection hardware, and real-time inventory integration ran roughly $2.4 million. For context, a conventional flagship of similar size costs $800K–$1.2M to build out. The premium is significant but not absurd, and it amortizes: the store can be "rebuilt" — new layout, new branding, new product focus — in an afternoon by pushing a config file. No contractors. No construction. No downtime.

The question isn't whether spatial retail is expensive. The question is whether rebuilding a physical store every three years is sane.

The studio projects that the flagship will reach cost parity with conventional retail build-outs within 18 months, assuming the space is reconfigured quarterly. After that, every reconfiguration is pure savings.

The Question Nobody's Asking

Every review of the flagship has focused on the technology. Is the rendering convincing? Is the latency acceptable? Does it feel real? These are the wrong questions. The technology works. The rendering is convincing. It feels real enough.

The right question is: what does it mean when a store can be anyone? A Propagation House flagship today. A partner brand's pop-up tomorrow. A competitor's concept the day after. When the store is software, the store is liquid. It flows toward whoever is willing to pay for the render time.

The spatial computing lab doesn't have an answer for this yet. But they're asking. And the fact that they're asking — rather than simply marveling at the walls — is why this matters beyond a single retail experiment.

The store is the screen now. The question is who's writing to it.
